\section{Autonomous Trading System – Policy, Execution and Risk Management}
\label{chap:ats-eval}

\subsection{Objectives and High-Level Design}

The Autonomous Trading System (ATS) is the final on-chain actor in the proposed architecture. Whereas the Wallets Collecting Module, Trader Selection Framework (TSF), Wallet Tracking Module, and Sentiment Analysis Module focus on \emph{observing} and \emph{interpreting} the market, the ATS is responsible for \emph{acting} on this information by placing and managing real trades on Solana-based decentralised exchanges.

At a conceptual level, the ATS receives a stream of enriched signals of the form
\[
    \big( i,\, \tau,\, C_{i}(\tau),\, G_{i}(\tau),\, z_{i}(\tau) \big),
\]
where $i$ is a token identifier, $C_{i}(\tau)$ is the raw coordination score, $G_{i}(\tau)$ is the sentiment-gated coordination score (Section~3.5.4), and $z_{i}(\tau)$ is a feature vector containing additional context (leader composition, sentiment scores, attention, regime labels, etc.). Based on these inputs, the ATS must decide whether to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Ignore the signal and take no action.
    \item Open a new position in token $i$.
    \item Adjust an existing position (e.g., scale in, scale out, tighten stops).
    \item Close a position partially or fully.
\end{itemize}

Crucially, the ATS is designed to be \emph{rule-based and transparent}. It does not attempt to learn an opaque trading policy from data; instead, it implements a configurable set of decision rules and risk limits that can be inspected, audited, and tuned by a human operator. This design choice aligns with the overall research goal: to investigate whether a pipeline that combines leader coordination, off-chain sentiment, and explicit risk controls can produce reasonable trading behaviour, without conflating this with the separate question of how to train an optimal policy.

\subsubsection{Core Objectives of the ATS}

The ATS is built around four core objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Translate enriched signals into concrete trade decisions.} The ATS must convert abstract features (e.g., $G_{i}(\tau)$, sentiment regime, leader composition) into actionable orders: which token to trade, in which direction, at what size, and with which exit conditions.

    \item \textbf{Execute trades reliably on decentralised exchanges.} Orders must be constructed, routed, and submitted to Solana DEX infrastructure (such as aggregators or routers) with appropriate handling of slippage, fees, and partial fills.

    \item \textbf{Manage portfolio-level and per-trade risk.} The ATS must enforce constraints on exposure (per token and overall), drawdowns, and position lifetimes. Risk constraints take precedence over new signal-triggered entries.

    \item \textbf{Support both simulation and live modes.} The same decision logic should be usable in an offline backtesting environment and in a live trading environment, differing only in the source of price/market data and the side effects of order execution.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{High-Level ATS Architecture}

To achieve these objectives, the ATS is structured into a small set of cooperating components. Figure~\ref{fig:ats-architecture} shows the high-level architecture.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Signal Ingestion and State Manager.} Listens to enriched signals from the upstream pipeline and maintains internal state about open positions, recent signals per token, and portfolio exposure. It converts raw messages into internal \emph{trade opportunities} that the policy engine can evaluate.

    \item \textbf{Policy and Decision Engine.} Encodes the core decision rules for entry, scaling, and exit. Given a trade opportunity and the current portfolio state, it decides whether to open or adjust a position and proposes a target position size, entry price constraints, and protective levels (e.g., stop-loss, take-profit).

    \item \textbf{Risk Manager.} Acts as a veto or modifier layer on top of the policy engine. It checks proposed actions against risk constraints (per-token caps, portfolio value-at-risk proxies, maximum number of concurrent positions, daily loss limits) and may reduce, defer, or cancel orders.

    \item \textbf{Execution Engine.} Translates approved trade actions into specific orders on Solana DEXs via an on-chain interface (for example, a router or aggregator). It handles slippage tolerances, time-in-force limits, partial fills, and retries where feasible.

    \item \textbf{Portfolio and P\&L Tracker.} Maintains a record of open positions, realised and unrealised P\&L, and key portfolio metrics. This component feeds both the Risk Manager (for enforcement) and the visualisation/UI layer used in the prototype application.

    \item \textbf{Backtesting and Replay Harness.} Provides an alternative execution path where, instead of sending real orders, the system replays historical price streams and simulates order fills according to a simplified market model. This harness is used to evaluate strategies and parameter settings without risking capital.
\end{itemize}

From a design perspective, the ATS is event-driven: enriched signals and market data updates are treated as events, and each event triggers a short decision cycle within the Policy Engine and Risk Manager. The separation between \emph{what} to do (policy) and \emph{whether it is allowed} (risk) keeps the system modular and makes it easier to reason about failures and edge cases.

\subsection{Signal Ingestion and Trade Decision Logic}

The first responsibility of the Autonomous Trading System (ATS) is to transform enriched signals
$\big(i, \tau, C_{i}(\tau), G_{i}(\tau), z_{i}(\tau)\big)$ into concrete \emph{trade opportunities}
and to decide, for each opportunity, whether to open, adjust, or close a position, or to ignore the signal.
This section describes how incoming signals are normalised, merged with the current portfolio state,
and processed by a rule-based decision engine.

\subsubsection{Signal Normalisation and De-duplication}

Enriched signals are delivered to the ATS via a message channel from the integration layer
(Section~3.5.4). Each signal record contains at least:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Token identifier $i$ (contract/mint address and symbol).
    \item Coordination score $C_{i}(\tau)$ and sentiment-gated score $G_{i}(\tau)$.
    \item Feature vector $z_{i}(\tau)$, including sentiment scores, attention, regime label, and
          leader-composition features.
    \item Timestamp $\tau$ and an internal unique identifier.
\end{itemize}

Upon receipt, the Signal Ingestion component performs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Schema validation and versioning.} Signals are first checked against the current
    schema version. If fields are missing or deprecated, the signal is either rejected or mapped to
    the closest compatible schema, with a warning logged.

    \item \textbf{Temporal ordering.} Signals are sorted or processed in non-decreasing order of
    $\tau$. In practice, minor out-of-order arrivals are tolerated by buffering small batches and
    resorting by timestamp.

    \item \textbf{De-duplication.} Signals that correspond to the same token and window
    (e.g., same token $i$ and coordination time $\tau$) may be generated multiple times in rare
    failure scenarios upstream. A short-lived cache keyed by $(i, \tau)$ is used to ignore exact or
    near-duplicate signals.

    \item \textbf{Context enrichment.} Before dispatching a signal to the Policy Engine, the
    ingestion layer attaches the current portfolio state for token $i$ (if any), including
    open quantity, average entry price, realised/unrealised P\&L, and risk flags. This yields an
    internal \emph{trade opportunity} object:
    \[
        \mathcal{O}_{i}(\tau) = \big(i, \tau, G_{i}(\tau), z_{i}(\tau),
        \text{position\_state}_{i}(\tau), \text{risk\_state}(\tau)\big).
    \]
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Position State and Opportunity Classification}

For each token $i$, the ATS maintains a simple, explicit position state:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{flat}: no open position in token $i$.
    \item \texttt{long}: a net long position is currently open (quantity $q_{i} > 0$).
    \item \texttt{closing}: an exit order is in progress (pending fills).
\end{itemize}

Given a trade opportunity $\mathcal{O}_{i}(\tau)$, the Policy Engine first classifies the opportunity
into one of several decision categories, based on $G_{i}(\tau)$, the sentiment regime, and the current
position state. Table~\ref{tab:ats-opportunity-types} summarises the main categories.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Trade opportunity types derived from enriched signals and position state.}
    \label{tab:ats-opportunity-types}
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.4cm} p{4.6cm} p{4.8cm}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Opportunity type} & \textbf{Conditions (simplified)} & \textbf{Intended action} \\
        \hline
        New long entry candidate &
        Position state \texttt{flat}; $G_{i}(\tau)$ above entry threshold; sentiment regime not in hard-block. &
        Consider opening a new long position with size determined by $G_{i}(\tau)$ and risk budget. \\
        \hline
        Scale-in candidate (add to long) &
        Position state \texttt{long}; $G_{i}(\tau)$ remains strong; risk budget allows higher exposure. &
        Consider increasing position size, subject to per-token and portfolio caps. \\
        \hline
        Hold / monitor &
        Position state \texttt{long}; $G_{i}(\tau)$ moderate or deteriorating; no strong exit trigger yet. &
        Maintain current position; update trailing stops or profit targets if appropriate. \\
        \hline
        Exit or de-risk candidate &
        Position state \texttt{long}; sentiment regime moves to caution or risk; or price/risk levels hit. &
        Consider partial or full exit; tighten stops and/or place take-profit orders. \\
        \hline
        Ignore / suppressed &
        Any state; $G_{i}(\tau)$ below minimum threshold or sentiment in hard-block regime. &
        Do not open or increase positions; optionally use as a signal to avoid future entries temporarily. \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

This classification provides a human-readable bridge between raw quantitative features and trading
actions. It is designed so that new rules can be added incrementally (for example, special handling
for tokens with unusually high attention $A_{i}^{\text{att}}(\tau)$).

\subsubsection{Entry and Scale-in Decision Rules}

For \emph{new long entry} and \emph{scale-in} candidates, the Policy Engine applies a set of
deterministic rules that map $G_{i}(\tau)$ and other features to a target position size and
price/risk parameters. A typical rule set includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Minimum gated strength.} Require
    \[
        G_{i}(\tau) \ge G_{\min}^{\text{entry}},
    \]
    where $G_{\min}^{\text{entry}}$ is a configurable threshold. Signals below this threshold are
    ignored for entry decisions.

    \item \textbf{Base position size.} Define a base risk unit $R$ (for example, a fraction of total
    portfolio value) and map $G_{i}(\tau)$ to a multiplier $m(G_{i}(\tau)) \in [0, m_{\max}]$,
    yielding a target notional exposure:
    \[
        \text{target\_notional}_{i}(\tau) = m\big(G_{i}(\tau)\big) \cdot R.
    \]
    The function $m(\cdot)$ can be piecewise linear or convex, placing more weight on very strong
    signals.

    \item \textbf{Incremental size for scale-in.} If a position already exists with notional
    $\text{current\_notional}_{i}$, the engine computes an incremental size:
    \[
        \Delta \text{notional}_{i}(\tau)
            = \max\big(0,\; \text{target\_notional}_{i}(\tau)
                               - \text{current\_notional}_{i}\big),
    \]
    subject to an upper cap per token. If $\Delta \text{notional}_{i}(\tau)$ is below a minimum trade
    size, no scale-in is performed.

    \item \textbf{Entry constraints (price and slippage).} The engine reads the current best bid/ask
    quotes from the DEX or router and sets:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item A maximum acceptable slippage percentage.
        \item A time-in-force window after which unfilled parts of the order are cancelled.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

If these rules yield a non-zero incremental size and the risk manager approves (Section~3.6.4),
the policy engine issues a \emph{proposed entry action} for token $i$ at time $\tau$, including
target notional, side, and protective levels (initial stop-loss and take-profit anchors).

\subsubsection{Exit and De-risking Rules}

Exit decisions are triggered by a combination of price-based conditions, sentiment and coordination
deterioration, and global risk constraints. The basic exit logic can be expressed in terms of three
layers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hard stops and take-profit levels.} For each open position, the ATS maintains:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item A stop-loss level, typically expressed as a percentage drop from the average entry price
        or as a maximum allowed loss per trade.
        \item A take-profit level, which may be static or adapted over time (e.g., trailing with
        favourable price moves).
    \end{itemize}
    Breach of either level automatically generates an exit action proposal, subject to execution
    constraints.

    \item \textbf{Signal-based de-risking.} Even if price has not hit stop-loss or take-profit levels,
    a sharp deterioration in coordination or sentiment can trigger partial or full exits. For example,
    if
    \[
        G_{i}(\tau) \le G_{\min}^{\text{hold}}
        \quad \text{or} \quad
        \text{Regime}_{i}(\tau) = \text{HardBlock},
    \]
    the policy engine may propose to close a fraction $\rho \in (0, 1]$ of the position, or to close
    it entirely.

    \item \textbf{Time-based exits.} To avoid indefinite holding of stale positions, the system can
    enforce a maximum holding period $T_{\max}^{\text{hold}}$. Positions older than this threshold
    are candidates for orderly exit, especially if fresh positive signals are absent.
\end{itemize}

Exit proposals, like entries, are then passed to the Risk Manager for validation against portfolio
constraints.

\subsubsection{Decision Cycle and Action States}

Putting these elements together, the ATS decision cycle for a single opportunity
$\mathcal{O}_{i}(\tau)$ consists of:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Ingest and normalise the signal; attach current position and risk state.
    \item Classify the opportunity type (new entry, scale-in, hold, exit candidate, ignore).
    \item Apply entry/scale-in rules or exit/de-risking rules, depending on the type.
    \item Generate a \emph{proposed action}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{OPEN\_LONG}(size, parameters),
        \item \texttt{INCREASE\_LONG}(increment, parameters),
        \item \texttt{CLOSE\_PARTIAL}(fraction, parameters),
        \item \texttt{CLOSE\_FULL}(parameters),
        \item or \texttt{NO\_ACTION}.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Submit the proposed action to the Risk Manager for validation and possible modification.
\end{enumerate}

position state to a proposed action that will be subject to risk checks and, if approved, executed
on-chain.

\subsection{Order Execution Engine and Exchange Integration}

Once the Policy Engine and Risk Manager agree on a proposed action (Section~3.6.2), the Order
Execution Engine is responsible for turning that action into concrete on-chain instructions. In the
prototype, execution is performed against Solana-based decentralised exchanges (DEXs) via a router
or aggregator that abstracts liquidity across multiple pools. The Execution Engine therefore sits at
the interface between high-level trading decisions and low-level protocol calls.

Its main responsibilities are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Translating proposed actions into specific order parameters (token pair, side, size, price
    constraints, slippage tolerance, time-in-force).
    \item Selecting an appropriate execution route (for example, a single pool or a multi-hop path
    chosen by an external router).
    \item Submitting transactions to the Solana network, monitoring their confirmation status, and
    reporting fills or failures back to the Portfolio Tracker.
    \item Applying basic safeguards against adverse execution, including slippage limits and
    cancellation of stale or partially filled orders.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Exchange Abstraction and Routing}

Rather than integrating directly with a single DEX protocol, the prototype assumes the presence of
an \emph{exchange abstraction layer} provided by a router or SDK. This layer exposes a simplified API:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Quote requests: given an input token, output token, and desired input or output amount,
    return an indicative price, expected slippage, and a proposed route.

    \item Swap execution: given a route and order parameters, construct and submit a transaction to
    the Solana network and return a transaction identifier.

    \item Transaction status: given a transaction identifier, report whether the transaction has been
    confirmed, partially filled, or failed.
\end{itemize}

The Execution Engine therefore treats the underlying DEX infrastructure as a black box with a
well-defined quote-and-swap interface. This makes it easier to swap providers (for example,
changing routers or adding fallback routes) without modifying the Policy or Risk modules.

For each approved action, the Execution Engine constructs a concrete order specification. Table~\ref{tab:ats-order-params} summarises the main parameters.

Before submitting an order, the Execution Engine requests a quote from the router using the desired
notional and slippage tolerance. If the quoted effective price or minimum expected output is
inconsistent with the assumptions used by the Policy Engine (for example, due to a thin order book
or sudden price movement), the order may be reduced in size or cancelled before it reaches the
chain.

\subsubsection{Execution Workflow: From Action to Transaction}

The execution workflow for a single approved action can be summarised as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Quote stage.} The Execution Engine sends a quote request to the router with the
    base/quote tokens, side (buy or sell), and desired input or output amount. The router responds
    with an indicative price, expected slippage, fee estimate, and a recommended route.

    \item \textbf{Sanity checks.} The engine verifies that:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The effective price does not exceed configured price bands (for example, no more than
        a certain percentage above the last observed market price for buys).
        \item The expected slippage is within the tolerance set by the Policy and Risk modules.
        \item The wallet has sufficient balance in the funding asset to cover notional + fees.
    \end{itemize}
    If any check fails, the order is rejected and logged as a ``blocked by execution constraints'' event.

    \item \textbf{Transaction construction and submission.} If checks pass, the engine asks the router
    to construct a swap transaction and submits it to the Solana network. The resulting transaction
    identifier is recorded in an execution log, along with the expected quantities and parameters.

    \item \textbf{Confirmation monitoring.} A lightweight polling or subscription mechanism monitors
    the status of the transaction until it reaches a final state (confirmed, failed, or expired).
    Confirmation includes reading back the actual filled amounts from on-chain logs.

    \item \textbf{Position and P\&L update.} Once the final state is known, the Portfolio Tracker
    updates the position for token $i$ (quantities, average entry/exit price) and records realised
    and unrealised P\&L. Any deviations between expected and actual fill (for example, partial fills)
    are propagated back to the Risk Manager and logged for later analysis.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Partial Fills, Failures, and Retry Logic}

On-chain execution is subject to a variety of failure modes: transactions may fail due to insufficient
liquidity, rapid price changes, network congestion, or router errors. The Execution Engine must
handle these cases without placing the system in an inconsistent state.

Key policies include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Partial fills.} If only a fraction of the desired quantity is filled, the Portfolio
    Tracker reflects the actual filled size. The remaining unfilled portion is either:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Cancelled (most conservative default), or
        \item Re-quoted as a new, smaller order if the Policy and Risk modules still approve.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Hard failures.} If a transaction is rejected or fails on-chain, the position state is
    left unchanged and the failure is logged with reason codes (where available). The engine may:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Attempt a single retry with more conservative parameters (smaller size, wider slippage)
        for entries, or
        \item Escalate to a ``risk alert'' if the failure affected an exit order that was intended to
        limit losses.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Timeouts.} If a transaction remains unconfirmed beyond a maximum time window, the
    engine treats it as expired and, where protocol-compatible, cancels any pending on-chain orders
    associated with it. The Policy Engine may then reconsider the trade opportunity based on updated
    prices.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Simulation Mode and Execution Abstraction}

For backtesting and simulation, the Execution Engine is replaced by a \emph{simulated execution
backend} that:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Consumes the same proposed actions as in live mode.
    \item Uses historical price streams to approximate fills (for example, assuming fills at the next
    bar close or within a bid/ask band).
    \item Applies simplified slippage and fee models.
\end{itemize}

Because both live and simulated modes share the same action interface, the Policy Engine and Risk
Manager can be evaluated without modification. This design also allows for incremental refinement
of the execution model (for example, more realistic slippage curves) without changing the core
decision logic.

\subsection{Risk Management and Position Sizing}

The Risk Manager is the central safety layer of the Autonomous Trading System (ATS). While the
Policy Engine proposes trades based on coordination and sentiment (Section~3.6.2), the Risk Manager
decides whether these trades are allowed under current exposure, drawdown, and capital constraints.
Its primary goal is to ensure that, even in the presence of strong signals, the system cannot exceed
predefined risk budgets at the per-trade, per-token, and portfolio levels.

Concretely, the Risk Manager:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Enforces hard limits on position sizes per token and on total portfolio exposure.
    \item Controls per-trade risk via maximum loss per position (stop-loss based) and minimum
    reward-to-risk ratios.
    \item Monitors realised and unrealised P\&L to detect drawdowns and apply temporary trading
    lockouts when losses exceed configured thresholds.
    \item Adjusts or vetoes proposed actions from the Policy Engine when they conflict with any of
    these constraints.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Risk Budgets and Exposure Constraints}

The system assumes that a total capital amount $V_{\text{port}}(\tau)$ (e.g., portfolio value in USDC)
is allocated to the strategy. Risk budgets are defined as fractions of this value. Table~\ref{tab:ats-risk-constraints}
summarises the main constraints.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Key risk and exposure constraints enforced by the Risk Manager.}
    \label{tab:ats-risk-constraints}
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.6cm} p{4.4cm} p{4.8cm}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Constraint} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
        \hline
        Per-trade risk budget $R$ &
        Maximum capital at risk per new trade (e.g., 0.5--2\% of $V_{\text{port}}$). &
        Bounds loss from any single position if its stop-loss is hit. \\
        \hline
        Max notional per token $L_{\text{token}}$ &
        Upper bound on total exposure in a single token (e.g., 5--10\% of $V_{\text{port}}$). &
        Reduces concentration risk in highly volatile assets. \\
        \hline
        Max aggregate exposure $L_{\text{gross}}$ &
        Upper bound on total gross long exposure across all tokens (e.g., 50--80\% of $V_{\text{port}}$). &
        Prevents full leverage or over-allocation to risky assets. \\
        \hline
        Max concurrent positions $N_{\max}$ &
        Maximum number of distinct tokens with open positions. &
        Limits operational complexity and diversification beyond a manageable set. \\
        \hline
        Daily loss limit $D_{\max}$ &
        Maximum allowed realised loss in a day; breaches trigger lockout. &
        Cuts off trading after unusually bad sessions. \\
        \hline
        Peak-to-valley drawdown limit $\Delta_{\max}$ &
        Maximum allowed portfolio drawdown from peak equity; breaches trigger lockout or de-risking. &
        Protects against slow but persistent underperformance. \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

At any time $\tau$, the Risk Manager computes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Current per-token exposure $E_{i}(\tau)$ (notional of open positions in token $i$).
    \item Total gross exposure $E_{\text{gross}}(\tau) = \sum_{i} E_{i}(\tau)$.
    \item Current realised P\&L for the trading day and peak-to-valley drawdown.
\end{itemize}

If a proposed action would cause any of the constraints in Table~\ref{tab:ats-risk-constraints} to be
violated, the Risk Manager either scales down the action or vetoes it altogether.

\subsubsection{Per-Trade Risk and Position Sizing}

The Policy Engine uses a base risk unit $R$ to propose target notionals
$\text{target\_notional}_{i}(\tau)$ (Section~3.6.2). The Risk Manager refines this by enforcing
per-trade loss limits and minimum reward-to-risk ratios.

Given a proposed entry or scale-in for token $i$, the following steps are applied:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Candidate stop-loss level.} The Policy Engine suggests an initial stop-loss price
    $P_{i}^{\text{SL}}$ for the position, based on volatility, liquidity, or simple percentage
    offsets. Combined with the intended entry price $P_{i}^{\text{entry}}$, this yields a per-unit
    loss:
    \[
        \ell_{i} = P_{i}^{\text{entry}} - P_{i}^{\text{SL}} \quad (\text{for long positions}).
    \]

    \item \textbf{Max permissible quantity from risk budget.} From the per-trade risk budget $R$, the
    maximum position size in units of the token is:
    \[
        q_{i}^{\max} = \frac{R}{\ell_{i}}.
    \]
    The corresponding notional exposure is $E_{i}^{\max} = q_{i}^{\max} \cdot P_{i}^{\text{entry}}$.

    \item \textbf{Apply per-token and portfolio caps.} The admissible additional exposure for token
    $i$ is:
    \[
        \Delta E_{i}^{\text{cap}}(\tau)
            = \min\big(
                L_{\text{token}} - E_{i}(\tau),\;
                L_{\text{gross}} - E_{\text{gross}}(\tau)
            \big),
    \]
    clamped below at 0 if either budget is already exhausted.

    \item \textbf{Final allowed exposure increment.} The Risk Manager compares:
    \[
        \Delta E_{i}^{\text{prop}}(\tau)
            = \text{proposed incremental notional from Policy Engine},
    \]
    with both $E_{i}^{\max}$ and $\Delta E_{i}^{\text{cap}}(\tau)$. The final allowed increment is:
    \[
        \Delta E_{i}^{\text{allowed}}(\tau)
            = \min\big(\Delta E_{i}^{\text{prop}}(\tau), E_{i}^{\max}, \Delta E_{i}^{\text{cap}}(\tau)\big).
    \]
    If $\Delta E_{i}^{\text{allowed}}(\tau)$ falls below a minimum trade size, the action is rejected.
\end{enumerate}

This ensures that:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The maximum potential loss on the trade (if the stop-loss is hit) does not exceed $R$.
    \item Per-token and portfolio exposure constraints are not breached.
\end{itemize}

A similar logic applies when scaling into existing positions, with $E_{i}(\tau)$ reflecting the
current exposure before the proposed increment.

\subsubsection{Portfolio-Level Risk and Lockout Mechanisms}

Beyond per-trade limits, the Risk Manager monitors portfolio-wide performance to prevent runaway
losses. Two key mechanisms are used:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Daily loss limit.} Let $L_{\text{day}}(\tau)$ be the cumulative realised loss for the
    current trading day (non-positive). If
    \[
        |L_{\text{day}}(\tau)| \ge D_{\max},
    \]
    the system enters a \emph{daily lockout} state: all new entries are rejected for the remainder of
    the day, and only risk-reducing exits are permitted. This emulates common discretionary trading
    practices.

    \item \textbf{Drawdown-based de-risking.} Let $V_{\text{peak}}$ denote the historical peak
    portfolio value and $V_{\text{port}}(\tau)$ the current value. The drawdown is:
    \[
        \Delta(\tau) = \frac{V_{\text{peak}} - V_{\text{port}}(\tau)}{V_{\text{peak}}}.
    \]
    If $\Delta(\tau) \ge \Delta_{\max}$, the system can either:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Enter a full lockout (no new entries, gradual exit of existing positions), or
        \item Reduce all position caps (e.g., halve $L_{\text{token}}$ and $L_{\text{gross}}$) until
        equity recovers above a threshold.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

These mechanisms are particularly important for experimental strategies in highly volatile markets,
where historical performance can change abruptly.

\subsubsection{Interaction with the Policy Engine}

The Risk Manager operates as a post-processor over proposed actions from the Policy Engine. Given a
proposed action $A_{i}(\tau)$ and associated parameters, the Risk Manager:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Computes updated exposure metrics and checks all constraints.
    \item Adjusts the action size if possible (e.g., reducing notional to remain within caps).
    \item Either returns a modified action for execution or a veto (\texttt{NO\_ACTION}) with a reason
    code (e.g., ``per-token cap exceeded'', ``daily loss limit hit'').
\end{enumerate}

In addition, the Risk Manager may pre-emptively propose \emph{risk-driven exits}, even in the absence
of fresh coordination or sentiment signals. For example, if aggregate exposure is high and a strong
negative portfolio move occurs, it can request partial de-risking across multiple tokens.

\subsection{Backtesting and Simulation Framework}

Before deploying the Autonomous Trading System (ATS) with real capital, its behaviour must be studied
under controlled conditions. The backtesting and simulation framework provides a way to replay
historical data and synthetic coordination/sentiment signals through the same decision logic used in
live trading, while replacing on-chain execution with a simplified market model. This allows
systematic evaluation of performance, robustness, and sensitivity to configuration choices.

\subsubsection{Objectives and Design Principles}

The backtesting environment is designed with the following objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reuse of production logic.} The Policy Engine, Risk Manager, and (simulated)
    Execution Engine share the same interfaces and code paths as in live mode, to avoid
    ``simulation-only'' shortcuts that would not hold in production.

    \item \textbf{Determinism and reproducibility.} Given the same historical data, configuration, and
    random seeds, the framework should produce identical results, enabling controlled experiments and
    parameter sweeps.

    \item \textbf{Transparency of assumptions.} The execution model (fills, slippage, latency) is kept
    deliberately simple and explicitly documented, so that evaluation results can be interpreted in
    light of these assumptions.

    \item \textbf{Rich logging for analysis.} All decisions (entries, exits, vetoes, lockouts) and
    their inputs (signals, sentiment, prices, risk state) are logged for post hoc analysis and
    debugging.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Data Inputs for Backtesting}

The backtesting framework consumes two main classes of input data:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Market price data.} Historical price series for tokens of interest, typically in
    the form of OHLCV candles (open, high, low, close, volume) at a fixed granularity (e.g., 1-minute
    or 5-minute bars). Where available, mid-price or bid/ask series can be used to model spread and
    slippage more realistically.

    \item \textbf{Signal and feature streams.} Recorded or simulated streams of enriched signals
    $\big(i, \tau, C_{i}(\tau), G_{i}(\tau), z_{i}(\tau)\big)$ produced by the upstream modules
    (wallet tracking and sentiment). In the simplest case, these are historical logs captured during
    a previous live or paper-trading run; in synthetic experiments, they can be generated from
    recorded on-chain events and offline sentiment analysis.
\end{itemize}

The backtester synchronises these inputs on a common time axis. At each simulation time step
(typically aligned to candle boundaries), it:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Injects all signals with timestamps in the current interval into the ATS.
    \item Updates the price and market state visible to the Policy Engine, Risk Manager, and simulated
    Execution Engine.
    \item Processes any pending orders and updates fills and P\&L based on the execution model.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Execution Model in Simulation}

In simulation mode, the Execution Engine is replaced by a backend that approximates order fills using
historical prices. Several levels of sophistication are possible; in this work, a simple but explicit
model is used:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fill price for market-style orders.} If the ATS issues a buy (long entry) at time
    $\tau$ and the next candle after $\tau$ has high/low range $[L, H]$, the simulated fill price is
    chosen as:
    \[
        P_{\text{fill}} = \min\big(H,\, P_{\text{entry}}^{\text{limit}} \cdot (1 + \text{slip}_{\max})\big),
    \]
    where $P_{\text{entry}}^{\text{limit}}$ is the indicative market price at time $\tau$ and
    $\text{slip}_{\max}$ is a configured slippage cap. If $L$ and $H$ do not cross the limit band,
    the order is treated as unfilled.

    \item \textbf{Stop-loss and take-profit triggers.} For open positions, if a subsequent candle has
    low $L \le P_{i}^{\text{SL}}$ or high $H \ge P_{i}^{\text{TP}}$, the corresponding stop-loss or
    take-profit is considered triggered. The fill price is approximated by the stop level or, in more
    conservative variants, by the worst price within the candle range.

    \item \textbf{Partial fills.} For simplicity, the prototype either fills the entire order or none
    of it within a candle. More advanced models could allow partial fills proportional to traded
    volume, but this is left for future work.
\end{itemize}

This model does not attempt to simulate full order-book dynamics, but it captures the basic
relationship between signals, execution timing, and candle-level price paths.

\subsection{Configuration, Hyperparameters, and Tuning Strategy}

The behaviour of the end-to-end system is governed by a large number of configuration parameters
and hyperparameters, spanning the Trader Selection Framework (TSF), Wallet Tracking Module,
Sentiment Analysis Module, and Autonomous Trading System (ATS). To keep the methodology
reproducible and interpretable, these parameters are grouped into structured configuration files
and tuned using a disciplined, scenario-based process.

This section summarises the main classes of hyperparameters relevant to the ATS and describes the
tuning strategy used in this project.

\subsubsection{Configuration Structure and Parameter Groups}

All configuration parameters are organised into a small set of logical groups, stored in human-readable
files (e.g., YAML) that are version-controlled alongside the code. For the purposes of the
methodology, four groups are especially important:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coordination and tracking parameters.} Control when the Wallet Tracking Module
    emits coordination signals (e.g., window length $\Delta T$, minimum leader count $n_{\min}$,
    minimum volume $V_{\min}$, weights in the coordination score $C_{i}(\tau)$).

    \item \textbf{Sentiment and gating parameters.} Control how off-chain sentiment is aggregated
    and how it gates coordination (e.g., sentiment thresholds $s_{\min}^{-}, s_{\max}^{+}$,
    red-flag thresholds $R_{\text{block}}$, shape of $\phi_{\text{pol}}$ and $\phi_{\text{risk}}$,
    source weights $\omega_{k}^{\text{sent}}$ and $\omega_{k}^{\text{risk}}$).

    \item \textbf{ATS trade policy parameters.} Control mapping from $G_{i}(\tau)$ and other features
    to entry, scale-in, and exit decisions (e.g., $G_{\min}^{\text{entry}}$, $G_{\min}^{\text{hold}}$,
    functions $m(G)$ for position sizing, stop-loss and take-profit offsets, maximum holding time
    $T_{\max}^{\text{hold}}$).

    \item \textbf{Risk and execution parameters.} Control per-trade risk budget $R$, per-token and
    portfolio caps $L_{\text{token}}, L_{\text{gross}}$, daily loss limit $D_{\max}$, drawdown
    limit $\Delta_{\max}$, maximum slippage and time-in-force, and simulated execution assumptions
    (e.g., slippage caps, fill rules).
\end{itemize}

primary influence on behaviour.

\subsubsection{Tuning Workflow Using the Backtesting Framework}

Hyperparameters are tuned using the backtesting framework described in Section~3.6.5. The tuning
process follows a pragmatic, multi-stage workflow:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Establish conservative defaults.} Start with parameter values derived from simple
    risk heuristics and prior experience (e.g., per-trade risk $R$ in the range 0.5--1\% of capital,
    modest per-token caps, tight slippage limits, relatively high thresholds for $n_{\min}$ and
    $G_{\min}^{\text{entry}}$).

    \item \textbf{Single-component sweeps.} Vary one parameter (or a small subset) at a time while
    keeping the rest fixed, to understand first-order effects. For example:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Sweep $G_{\min}^{\text{entry}}$ to see how it affects trade frequency, hit rate, and
        average win/loss.
        \item Sweep $R$ and $L_{\text{token}}$ to understand sensitivity of drawdowns and total return
        to risk per trade and concentration.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Scenario comparisons.} Run the experimental scenarios described in
    Section~3.6.5 (coordination-only baseline, no-risk-controls variant, full system) to quantify the
    incremental impact of sentiment gating and risk caps.

    \item \textbf{Coarse grid or random search over critical parameters.} For a small subset of
    critical parameters (e.g., $G_{\min}^{\text{entry}}$, $R$, $L_{\text{token}}$, $D_{\max}$),
    perform a coarse grid search or random search over plausible ranges and evaluate performance
    metrics (Table~\ref{tab:ats-metrics}). This helps identify regions where the trade-off between
    return and drawdown is favourable.

    \item \textbf{Stability checks.} For promising parameter configurations, repeat backtests on
    different time segments or different token universes to check whether performance is robust or
    highly regime-dependent.

    \item \textbf{Final selection and documentation.} Select a configuration that offers an acceptable
    compromise between performance and risk, prioritising robustness over maximum historical return.
    The chosen hyperparameters are recorded with a unique configuration identifier and committed to
    version control.
\end{enumerate}

Throughout this workflow, emphasis is placed on avoiding overfitting: parameters are not tuned to
optimise performance on a single historical period or set of tokens, but rather to exhibit reasonable
behaviour across multiple scenarios.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity and Ablation Analysis}

Beyond tuning, the backtesting framework is used for sensitivity and ablation analyses that directly
relate to the research questions. Examples include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to sentiment thresholds.} Examine how varying $R_{\text{block}}$ and
    $s_{\min}^{-}$ affects the frequency with which sentiment vetoes coordinated tokens and how this
    impacts overall performance and risk. This helps quantify the contribution of the sentiment
    layer.

    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to per-trade risk $R$.} Evaluate how different per-trade risk budgets
    influence drawdown profiles and whether returns scale approximately linearly with risk, or whether
    higher risk budgets disproportionately increase tail losses.

    \item \textbf{Ablation of risk caps.} Temporarily relax or remove certain risk constraints
    (e.g., $L_{\text{token}}$ or $D_{\max}$) to show how much they reduce drawdowns and whether they
    significantly limit upside. This supports an argument that risk management is not merely an
    implementation detail but a key part of the methodology.
\end{itemize}

The results of these analyses are presented in the Evaluation chapter, but their methodology is
rooted in the configuration and backtesting design described here.

\subsubsection{Configuration Management and Reproducibility}

To ensure that experimental results can be reproduced and audited, all configurations are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stored as versioned configuration files (e.g., YAML) in the same repository as the code.
    \item Tagged with human-readable identifiers (e.g., run IDs, scenario names) and referenced in
          backtest logs and result tables.
    \item Logged at runtime: each backtest or live run writes its effective configuration (including
          defaulted and derived parameters) into a run-specific artefact directory.
\end{itemize}

loop: candidate configurations are passed to the backtesting harness, which returns performance and
risk metrics; these are then analysed to inform updated configuration choices, closing the loop.
